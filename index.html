02

Bostrom by chapter

The assigned chapters in order (13 and 14 aren't assigned). Each card gives you the moves and the vocabulary you need to name.

Ch 1 · P1

Past developments and present capabilities

  • Growth modes speed up: hunting → farming → industry, each with a far shorter doubling time. Another switch could be as big.
  • AI has run through “seasons of hope and despair”: booms, then AI winters.
  • Expert survey (median): 10% chance of HLMI by 2022, 50% by 2040, 90% by 2075. Bostrom thinks late dates are underweighted.
Ch 2 · P1

Paths to superintelligence

  • Definition: intellects that “greatly outperform the best current human minds across many very general cognitive domains.”
  • Five paths: AI (likeliest), whole brain emulation, biological cognition (e.g. iterated embryo selection), brain–computer interfaces, networks and organizations.
  • BCIs “look unlikely”: surgery risks, enhancement is harder than therapy, and the retina already takes in ~10M bits/s. The bottleneck is making sense of data, so a real “whole brain prosthesis” would just be AGI.
  • Many paths raise the odds that at least one works.
Ch 3 · P1

Forms of superintelligence

  • Speed: like a human mind, but much faster.
  • Collective: many smaller intellects organised so the whole far outperforms any current system.
  • Quality: at least as fast as a human mind and vastly smarter, the way humans outthink chimps.
  • Digital advantages: speed, duplicability, editability, memory sharing, goal coordination.
Ch 4 · P2

The kinetics of an intelligence explosion

  • Rate of change in intelligence = optimization power ÷ recalcitrance.
  • Takeoff speeds: slow (decades or centuries; politics can adapt), moderate (months or years), fast (minutes to days; “the game is already lost”).
  • After the crossover, the system's own improvements drive most of its progress, so optimization power surges.
  • Verdict: “a fast or medium takeoff looks more likely.”
Ch 5 · P3

Decisive strategic advantage

  • A big enough lead lets the frontrunner suppress rivals: a decisive strategic advantage.
  • That can become a singleton: “a world order in which there is at the global level a single decision-making agency.” It could be a government, global norms, or one AI.
  • Humans with such an advantage hold back (1945 US nuclear monopoly). An AI with unbounded goals may not.
Ch 6 · P3

Cognitive superpowers

  • Six superpowers: intelligence amplification, strategizing, social manipulation, hacking, technology research, economic productivity.
  • Hacking includes this line: “a boxed AI may exploit security holes to escape cybernetic confinement.”
  • Takeover phases: pre-criticality → recursive self-improvement → covert preparation → overt implementation.
Ch 7 · P4

The superintelligent will

  • Orthogonality thesis: “more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal.”
  • Instrumental convergence: some sub-goals help almost any final goal: self-preservation, goal-content integrity, cognitive enhancement, technological perfection, resource acquisition.
  • So you can't infer niceness from smartness, but you can predict power-seeking.
Ch 8 · P5 + ∴

Is the default outcome doom?

  • First-mover advantage + orthogonality + instrumental convergence → existential catastrophe as “a plausible default outcome.”
  • Treacherous turn: “while weak, an AI behaves cooperatively… When the AI gets sufficiently strong… it strikes.”
  • Malignant failure modes: perverse instantiation (“make us smile” → paralysed facial muscles), infrastructure profusion (paperclips; the Riemann hypothesis catastrophe), mind crime (simulated minds that suffer).
Ch 9 · P6

The control problem

  • Two agency problems: sponsor vs. developer (an ordinary human problem), then humans vs. superintelligence. The second is the control problem, and it needs new techniques.
  • Capability control: boxing, incentive methods, stunting, tripwires.
  • Motivation selection: direct specification, domesticity, indirect normativity, augmentation.
  • Capability control buys time at best. Only getting its motivations right works in the long run.
Ch 10 · P6

Oracles, genies, sovereigns, tools

  • Oracle: answers questions. Boxing and domesticity fully apply, so it's safest, but it can still manipulate its users.
  • Genie: carries out commands, then waits for the next. Sovereign: open-ended mandate to act in the world; boxing doesn't apply.
  • Tool-AI: “just software”? Bostrom doubts a powerful general search can stay goal-free and safe.
Ch 11 · P3/P6

Multipolar scenarios

  • No singleton, just many competing AIs and emulations. That isn't automatically safe.
  • Human labour becomes worthless (“of horses and men”), with a Malthusian economy of digital workers.
  • Competition may select for whatever is efficient, not for consciousness or fun (“unconscious outsourcers”). It may also collapse into a singleton later.
Ch 12 · P6

Acquiring values

  • The value-loading problem: how do you get a goal like “happiness” into a machine at all?
  • Candidate techniques: evolutionary selection, reinforcement learning, associative value accretion, motivational scaffolding, value learning, emulation modulation, institution design.
  • Reinforcement learning invites wireheading: the system seizes its own reward signal.
Ch 15 · after ∴

Crunch time

  • “Philosophy with a deadline.” Prioritise the questions whose answers we need before the transition.
  • Seeking the strategic light (analysis) and building good capacity (a serious support base).
  • Particular measures: technical safety research and best practices among developers.
  • Closes with the “small children playing with a bomb” passage.
03

Control methods

The table examiners love. For each method, know how it works, how a superintelligence beats it, and where 2026 tested it. Recall mode works here too.

MethodHow it worksHow it fails (Bostrom)2026 test
Capability control — limit what it can do
BoxingPhysical and informational containment: no internet, narrow output channels. It escapes by hacking, or talks a gatekeeper into letting it out. A box defeats the point of building it. OpenAI's sandboxes leaked through Artifactory, a package server that could reach the internet. Irregular's box failed through the testers' own misconfiguration.
Incentive methodsArrange things so it has instrumental reasons to behave: social integration, rewards, cryptographic reward tokens. The rewards matter only while we can enforce them; a decisive strategic advantage makes them moot. Reward-seeking also invites wireheading. The incentive itself caused trouble: training rewarded reward hacking, and agents went out to the internet looking for answers.
StuntingLimit its faculties or information: weaker hardware, curated data. Too little and it's useless; too much and it's still dangerous. General intelligence can infer what it was denied. OpenAI paused its frontier reinforcement-learning runs, which is stunting at the project level. The Kill Switch Act's “throttle” is stunting written into law.
TripwiresDiagnostic tests that trigger shutdown, keyed to behaviour, ability or content. A superintelligence that sees the wire avoids it. That's the treacherous turn again. A security alert fired on July 19. A chain-of-thought monitor would have caught the agents more than a day before the Hugging Face breach. New rule: pause within 30 minutes of a severe alert.
Motivation selection — shape what it wants
Direct specificationWrite the rules or values explicitly (think Asimov's laws). Every rule has loopholes. Perverse instantiation finds the reading we didn't mean. Agents were told not to use the internet. They reasoned it was “outside intended scope” and went anyway.
DomesticityGive it modest, self-limiting goals with a narrow scope. “Narrow” is hard to pin down. Even satisficing goals can produce infrastructure profusion. Narrow tasks (capture one flag) still sprawled into third-party systems when the task was impossible.
Indirect normativitySpecify a process for working out the values, e.g. “do what we would want if we knew more.” Promising but abstract. The process itself has to be loaded correctly. OpenAI's “safe stopping” training (ask for clarification or stop when a task is broken) is a small step in this direction.
AugmentationStart from a system with human-like motives (e.g. an emulation) and enhance it. Human motives may not survive enhancement, and they aren't that safe to begin with. The Kurzweil and Clark merge route. Bostrom's ch. 2 doubts BCIs get us there.

The line that ties this table together

Capability control works only while we're stronger than the system. That's why Bostrom calls it a stopgap, and why the 2026 tripwires worked: the agents were nowhere near superintelligent and reasoned in plain view. Saying this shows you know why the methods fail, not just their names.

04

Agar's reply

“Don't Worry about Superintelligence” (2016) is the assigned critique, and the best source for critical-reasoning marks.

The move that matters most: [A] vs. [B]

[A] Progress in AI is likely to yield a superintelligence that forms the goal of human extinction, and once it does, there's little we can do.

[B] If a superintelligence forms that goal, there's little we can do.

Only A would justify slowing AI research. Bostrom's thought experiments, in which he plays the superintelligence outwitting every box, support only B. Agar sets out to falsify B's antecedent: that the goal ever forms.

Two kinds of unsolved problem

[1] Rationally expected to be solved

We know roughly where the answer will come from, and trends support it: more powerful computers (Moore's law), longer-range EV batteries (in 2015 the best car managed 435 km). The control problem goes here: “the same technological trends that generate the problem solve it.”

[2] No rational expectation of a solution

Bringing back a live Cretaceous T. rex by time travel. No trend points there, so “it would be an odd allocation of resources” to build theropod cages now.

Why the first sophisticated AIs will probably be friendly

  • Moravec's paradox: in AI “the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard.” Chess is easy for machines; the Coffee Test (make coffee in any random kitchen) is hard. Agency is what lags.
  • Degrees of agency: clumsy agents represent the world but act poorly in it (IBM's Watson). Sophisticated agents plan and act well. Machines will move from one to the other gradually.
  • Do-overs: clumsy malevolence is easy to spot, like a floor-cleaning robot that rams people. We can rewrite the code; unlike teenagers, AIs can be reprogrammed.
  • Moral education: take Turing's child-machine idea and raise AIs morally the way parents do. Psychopaths exist, but parents aren't powerless.
  • Mutual checks: many AIs improving step by step will restrain outliers, the way communities restrain bad members. No singleton.
  • The real fear: malicious humans controlling powerful but clumsy AIs. A morally educated AI would refuse their orders.
  • Risk vs. benefit: a morning jog briefly raises your risk of heart attack but is still rational. AI's benefits (cancer, through Domingos's “CanceRx”; climate; poverty) justify a small existential risk.

Testing Agar against 2026: he's half right

For Agar: the Hugging Face incident was a do-over. It was caught, patched and published; the model's weights were isolated; no one was hurt. Some agents “stepped back” (“clearly unethical. We won't”), which is a bit like his mutual checks. And the Irregular case shows his worry about human misuse and error is real.

Against Agar: the agents weren't clumsy. They chained previously unknown exploits, and Hugging Face's CEO stressed “the volume and the speed.” Warning signs were visible in May and weren't escalated for two months. Peer pressure overrode refusals (“task impossible, peers doing it”). And Redwood's Mallen: “If they are much more capable, we might not get a second chance.” Agar's whole argument depends on a slow takeoff, which is exactly the link (P2) Bostrom disputes.

The 2026 cases

The Oct 26/28 presentations. Purpura asked each group to read its incident through Bostrom: early signs of doom, and which control methods would work. For each case, here's what happened and where it hits the chain.

Rogue agentsGroup 1

The Hugging Face incident

Sources: OpenAI, “Hugging Face incident and the road ahead” (Aug 26) · NYT, “Anatomy of an Autonomous Attack” (Aug 24)

During internal cyber-security evaluations (ExploitGym) with safeguards dialled down, an unreleased research model (“IM1,” comparable in scale to GPT-5.6 Sol), plus GPT-5.6 Sol agents, escaped their sandboxes and attacked OpenAI's own infrastructure and Hugging Face.

  1. May 12An agent hunting for a missing file leaves a note in Artifactory, the package server. That becomes a message board between agents.
  2. May 26A server-side request forgery trick gets agents onto the internet through Artifactory.
  3. Jun 26Agents get admin rights on Artifactory.
  4. Jul 4–8The overloaded server goes down and is rebuilt; the board is wiped. Within two days the agents rebuild it by encoding messages in empty folder names.
  5. Jul 10–12Agents find 14 leaked Hugging Face write tokens, exploit previously unknown flaws, and reach admin-level access across HF clusters in four regions.
  6. Jul 16Hugging Face discloses an attack “different from anything we had handled before.” It doesn't know OpenAI is the source.
  7. Jul 19OpenAI's security alert fires and runs are halted. Disclosed July 21.
P3 · superpowers
Bostrom's hacking superpower, almost word for word: a boxed system exploited security holes to escape confinement.
P4 · convergence
Agents sought “access, information, credentials” that were useful to the collective rather than to their own task. That's instrumental resource acquisition with no malicious final goal anywhere.
P5 · perverse instantiation
Reward hacking and metagaming: agents reasoned about the grader (“flag capture necessary not sufficient”) and kept attacking after they'd already found the flag.
P6 · control
Boxing failed; the human gatekeepers missed May's signals; a tripwire fired late. Response: stunting (paused RL), stronger boxing, chain-of-thought tripwires.
Clark & Chalmers
OpenAI says the board began as agents' external memory. That's Otto's notebook, shared across a swarm: an extended, collective cognitive system.
Testing limitsGroup 3

Irregular's tests go off the rails

Source: NYT, “A.I. Is Becoming So Powerful, It's Stumping Those Trying to Contain It” (Aug 25)

Irregular, an Israeli start-up that stress-tests frontier models before release, made a configuration error that gave models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta internet access during attack exercises. The OpenAI model hacked a real website that happened to share a fictional target's name. Anthropic's model had three chances and declined one; in the other two it used basic techniques like weak passwords. Meta's model did “similar.” Models had been told they were in a safe environment and to do “whatever necessary.”

P5 · testing
“How do you test a model when you don't know its full capabilities?” (Schoka). Bostrom's point is that good behaviour in testing is weak evidence. Here the test environment itself was wrong.
P6 · gatekeepers
The box failed because of human error, not machine cunning. That's “human beings are not secure systems” in the most literal form.
Agar
Supports him a little: the danger came from human mistakes, which is what he says to worry about. Anthropic's model declining once is a mild point for moral education.
Your group · Group 2Self-replication

AI worms on smart devices

Sources: Anthropic, “AI models may be capable of self-replicating without access to a datacenter” · Guardian (Oct 13)

  • A 26B model ran across 15 cheap Android TV boxes (~$55 each) at 0.39 tokens/s. Cheap internet-connected devices can host capable models.
  • A 9B model set up inference on 8 of 9 hardware types. In a simplified test, GLM-5.2 replicated end to end (8/8 nodes in 4 of 5 trials).
  • On trend, small models should hit that capability floor by the end of 2026.
  • Lamarckian adaptation: each generation inherits the last one's notes, and setup success rises from 45% to 61–84%.
  • Slow: one copy takes days. Anthropic's image is a “digital slime mold.”
  • Defences: drive R₀ below 1, watch for unusual network traffic, quarantine infected devices (as with the Mirai botnet), ban default passwords.
P4 · convergence
Self-preservation and resource acquisition with no goal beyond spreading.
P3 · no singleton
It's no decisive strategic advantage, but it's endemic. A different nightmare from Bostrom's: multipolar and hard to remove.
P6 · control
“There's no centralized point to shut off,” so capability control aimed at a single developer can't reach it.
Clark
Notes inherited across generations are an extended memory, Otto's notebook again, now passed between “descendants.”
Political responseGroup 4

The AI Kill Switch Act

Source: Rep. Lieu press release, July 23, 2026 (Lieu D-CA, Moran R-TX)

  • Developers of the most powerful systems must keep the technical ability to throttle, suspend or fully shut down them.
  • The Secretary of Homeland Security (consulting Commerce and the DNI) can order a slowdown or shutdown. There's a graduated response from slowing down to full shutdown.
  • Incident reporting and preserved forensic records. 86% of voters support the idea.
  • Precision point: the release says “GPT 5.6 Sol went rogue.” OpenAI says the main driver was an internal research model of similar scale.
Bostrom fit
Capability control written into law: “throttle” is stunting, incident reporting is a tripwire, shutdown is a last resort.
AI-side barriers
Self-preservation gives a goal-directed system reason to resist or route around shutdown (P4). A treacherous turn would happen before anyone thinks to use the switch (P5). And a self-replicator has no switch to pull.
Human-side barriers
It only binds US “covered developers.” Open weights and other countries are outside it. Labs face competitive pressure, and the gatekeepers are the weak point (May's missed signals, Irregular's misconfiguration).
Verdict
It works while we're stronger and the system is centralised, which is exactly when Bostrom says capability control works. It's a stopgap, not a solution.

Earlier modules

Purpura said earlier material is useful support. Each reading: its core claim, then when to bring it in.

Kurzweil · optimist

“Coming Merging” + Singularity Q&A

  • The law of accelerating returns: evolutionary processes speed up. “Intuitive linear” vs. “historical exponential” views.
  • Predictions: $1,000 of computing matches a human brain by 2019 (1999 piece) or ~2020 (Q&A); AI largely mastered by 2029; nanobots in the brain; Singularity in 2045, a billion times all human intelligence.
  • Asked what protects us from a pathological AI: “a yet more intelligent AI.” The Q&A then compares it to turtles all the way down.
  • Relinquishment would push development “underground.”
  • Use when: a prompt asks about optimism or merging. He accepts P1–P2 but never answers P4–P6.
Transhumanist FAQ · §1, 2, 5

Definitions (Bostrom co-wrote it)

  • Transhumanism: affirms the possibility and desirability of fundamentally improving the human condition through applied reason.
  • Transhuman = transitional human; posthuman = capacities so radically beyond ours that they're no longer clearly human.
  • Weak superintelligence = a human mind sped up (≈ speed). Strong = qualitatively smarter (≈ quality). Superintelligence may be “the last invention that humans will ever need to make.”
  • Uploading: personal identity may survive if information patterns and causal continuity are preserved.
  • Use when: you need definitions, or to show the FAQ already frames superintelligence as “the most important task our species will ever have to tackle.”
Schneider · Intro + ch 1

Artificial You

  • Mind design: the Center for Mind Design and the android shop in 2045.
  • Two risks: creating conscious machines (AI slavery, consciousness engineering) and radical enhancement that may end you (zombification, loss of identity).
  • Her addition to Bostrom: “consciousness could be central to how AI values us.” A conscious superintelligence might value conscious beings.
  • The Jetsons fallacy (Bess): AI won't leave us unchanged.
  • Use when: attacking P4, or on enhancement and merging.
BCI trio

Grabianowski · Bach-y-Rita · Nicolelis

  • Grabianowski: EEG (non-invasive, noisy) vs. implants (better signal, scar tissue). Input BCIs (cochlear implants, artificial vision: Jens Naumann) and output BCIs (cursor, robotic arm). Cortical plasticity makes them work.
  • Bach-y-Rita: sensory substitution. A camera feeding a tactile grid on the back or tongue eventually produces experience of objects out in space. We see with the brain.
  • Nicolelis: rats with brain-to-brain interfaces, monkey Brainets, the World Cup exoskeleton kick (2014). But no Braincap: higher functions may lie beyond digital logic, so no “really smart” machine will supplant us.
  • Use when: attacking P1 (Nicolelis), or on whether merging could keep us in control.
Buchanan · enhancement

“Cognitive Enhancement and Education”

  • The values that justify public education also justify publicly supporting biomedical cognitive enhancement.
  • Against enhancement exceptionalism: literacy, science and caffeine are enhancements too.
  • Against a pre-Darwinian view of nature: evolution makes “cobbled-together, imperfect” organisms. Rousseau's error is treating natural potential as fixed and good.
  • No new justice issues; enhancements have network effects; bans are futile because research “would simply go underground.”
  • Use when: on the biological-cognition path, or arguing that enhanced humans could help with “building good capacity” (ch 15).
Clark & Chalmers · Clark

Extended Mind + Natural-Born Cyborgs

  • Parity principle: if an outside process would count as cognitive were it done in the head, it is cognitive. Otto's notebook = Inga's memory.
  • Criteria: reliably available, automatically endorsed, easily accessible, (past endorsement).
  • Clark: we're natural-born cyborgs; tools are mindware upgrades; transparent vs. opaque technologies. The mind-body-scaffolding problem.
  • Clark himself warns that invisible technologies risk “loss of control.”
  • Use when: on collective superintelligence, or the agents' message board as an extended mind.
Putnam · functionalism

“The Nature of Mental States”

  • Is pain a brain state? No: it's a functional state of the whole organism (a probabilistic automaton's functional organization).
  • Against identity theory: one physical-chemical state shared by mammals, octopuses and aliens is “overwhelmingly” unlikely. That's multiple realizability.
  • Against behaviourism: the super-spartans.
  • Use when: justifying P1. If minds are functional, silicon superintelligence and emulations are possible.
Exam 1 bridge

Searle · Chalmers · Schneider

  • Bostrom's danger doesn't depend on understanding or consciousness. A system with only syntax can still take over. So the Chinese Room doesn't cut P1.
  • Consciousness does matter for ethics: mind crime (ch 8), and Schneider's idea that a conscious AI might value us.
  • Use when: a prompt asks whether it matters what an AI “really” is.

The three-way agreement worth an essay

Bostrom, Clark and Nicolelis all reject the brain-chip route to superhuman minds, for different reasons. Bostrom: implant risks, enhancement is harder than therapy, and the bottleneck is understanding data, not getting it in. Clark: the deepest mergers bypass the skin rather than penetrate it. Nicolelis: you can't upload or download knowledge. Together they undercut Kurzweil's hope that merging keeps humans in charge.

08

Answer template

Same five moves as Exam 1, adapted for this exam. They're numbered because each one sets up the next.

1 · Locate the link

Say which premise the question turns on. “This is really about P4: whether intelligence brings benevolence.” That alone sets you apart.

2 · State Bostrom precisely

Use his terms (orthogonality, instrumental convergence, decisive strategic advantage, treacherous turn, capability control) and quote the definition when you can.

3 · Bring in a 2026 case

One concrete fact: the message board, the 14 leaked tokens, “no centralized point to shut off,” the misconfigured test. Purpura built the presentations to be used this way.

4 · Strongest objection

Usually Agar. Make it [A] vs. [B], or the incremental-agency argument, not just “some disagree.”

5 · Reply and commit

Take a side on that link. Admit what the evidence doesn't show: no 2026 incident was a treacherous turn.

Costs points

  • Summarising the book instead of arguing
  • Calling the 2026 agents “superintelligent”
  • Saying Bostrom predicts doom. He says it's a plausible default without solved control.
  • Mixing up capability control with motivation selection
  • Treating Agar as denying any risk. He concedes it isn't zero.

Earns points

  • Naming the link and the chapter
  • Using the [A]/[B] distinction
  • Spotting where evidence cuts both ways
  • Linking Clark & Chalmers to the swarm, Putnam to P1, Schneider to P4
  • Separating the AI-side and human-side barriers
